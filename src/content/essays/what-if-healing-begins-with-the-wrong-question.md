A few weeks ago, someone shared a video in a men's group I belong to. It asked whether healing can become an identity of its own, a life spent preparing to live rather than living.

The idea stayed with me. I could recognise the weariness behind it, the feeling that there is always another wound to uncover, another pattern to understand, another part of ourselves that must be fixed before we are finally ready. But as I sat with it, a different question appeared underneath the first one.

What if healing begins with an assumption that is worth questioning?

The assumption is that because we carry pain, trauma, fear or difficult patterns, something essential within us must be broken. Once that premise is accepted, the rest follows quite naturally. We search for the cause, name the wound, work on the pattern and hope to arrive at a version of ourselves that is finally repaired.

There can be enormous value in that work. Understanding what happened to us can bring language to confusion. Therapy, grief, support and honest reflection can help us recognise patterns that hurt us and the people around us. Taking responsibility for our behaviour matters. None of that is in question here.

The question sits one layer deeper. How do we know that pain is proof of a defective self?

Pain tells us that something hurts. Fear may tell us that something feels unsafe. A repeated pattern may show us where experience, protection and habit have become tangled together. These things may need care, attention and change. But it does not necessarily follow that our essential nature has been damaged or that we must become someone else before we can fully enter our own lives.

I cannot prove that we are essentially whole. But brokenness is also an interpretation, not a measurement. We rarely pause to examine it because it has become so embedded in the language of self-improvement. We speak of fixing ourselves, working on ourselves, overcoming ourselves and becoming better versions of ourselves. Even the gentler language of healing can quietly preserve the same verdict.

There is still something wrong with me. There is still somewhere else I need to get.

Names and explanations can be useful here. Discovering that a reaction has a history may replace shame with understanding. Recognising a pattern can create enough space to choose differently. The danger is not in naming what has happened. It is in allowing the explanation to become a total description of who we are. A map that helps us find our way through one part of our experience can quietly become the boundary of the whole life.

The same can happen with the identity of being someone who is healing. At first it may offer companionship, language and relief. Over time, though, every ordinary difficulty can begin to look like another layer that must be excavated. Sadness becomes evidence of unfinished work. Uncertainty becomes a sign that another answer is hidden somewhere in the past. Living is postponed until the inner work produces a person who no longer struggles.

That is the point at which a healing practice can begin to resemble the pressure it was meant to relieve. The method may be gentler, but the message underneath has not changed. You are not there yet.

I see a similar pattern in Tai Chi. A student learns the short form and immediately wants to learn the long form. Then another weapon, another sequence or another level becomes the next sign of progress. There is nothing wrong with learning more. Curiosity and ambition can be joyful. But it is easy to mistake accumulation for depth and completion for understanding.

I sometimes tell students that I could show them one thing in an hour that would give them enough to explore for the rest of their lives. They could learn to breathe. They could practise Tai Chi walking. Neither has a finish line. The practice keeps revealing itself because the person practising keeps changing.

Strangely, my own Tai Chi improved when I became less concerned with reaching a particular point. I did not stop practising or caring about the quality of the practice. I stopped asking the practice to prove that I had arrived. Without that pressure, there was more room to notice, soften and respond.

There are still corrections. A stance can be unstable, the breath can become forced and unnecessary tension can hide in the shoulders or hips. Seeing those things clearly is part of the practice. But a correction is not a condemnation of the person. It is simply contact with what is happening now. The work becomes more precise when it is no longer carrying the additional burden of proving our worth.

Perhaps healing can be approached in the same way. It can be a practice of meeting what is here without turning the person who is meeting it into a failed project. We can understand an old fear without making the fear our identity. We can change harmful behaviour without concluding that our deepest nature is harmful. We can seek help without making our need for help evidence against our worth.

This is not a case for passivity. Wholeness does not mean that every choice is healthy, every relationship should continue or every wound will resolve itself. It does not remove consequences, boundaries or responsibility. A person can be whole and still cause harm. A person can be whole and still need care. A person can be whole and still have a great deal to learn.

That distinction matters because the place from which change begins affects the change itself. If I begin with self-rejection, improvement can become an attempt to escape myself. Each new insight offers temporary relief, followed by another reason I am not yet ready. Even remembering that I am whole can become one more task to complete correctly.

If I begin with the possibility of wholeness, growth has a different quality. I am not trying to manufacture a worthy self. I am learning to live more honestly as the person already here. The work may be just as demanding, but it is no longer organised around the belief that life must wait until I am fixed.

This goes against much of what surrounds us. A great deal of modern life depends on keeping us dissatisfied with who we are, how we look, what we have achieved and how far we have progressed. There is always another level, method or version of ourselves just beyond reach. The chase can feel responsible and even noble because it wears the clothes of growth.

Yet nature does not seem embarrassed by becoming. A tree grows, loses leaves, bends in weather and carries the marks of what it has lived through. The marks matter, but they do not make the tree a failed tree. Its growth is not a journey away from itself.

I offer that only as an image, not as proof. Human lives are more complicated, and suffering is not made simple by comparison. But the image helps me hold another possibility. Perhaps we can change without declaring war on what we are. Perhaps healing is not the construction of a better self, but a way of becoming more able to inhabit this life.

The movement may not be from brokenness to perfection. It may be from forgetting to remembering, and even that remembering may come and go. The point is not to hold onto a permanent state or turn wholeness into a new achievement. It is to question the sentence we may have accepted before the work even began.

What if you are not a problem to be solved? What if the parts of you that need care, honesty and change exist within a life that is already worthy of being lived?
