The first time softness really made sense to me in Tai Chi was not as gentleness, but as information.

If I stiffen in push hands, I lose the line of force almost immediately. If I stay soft enough to feel, direction becomes clearer, timing improves, and I do not need nearly as much effort to respond well.

That changed how I notice working rooms too. A difficult update lands, a senior voice tightens, someone gives the polished version instead of the accurate one, and the conversation starts protecting itself. The meeting still looks controlled. What disappears is sensitivity.

People can keep exchanging words while the useful information arrives already edited. Nuance gets flattened. Risk arrives late. It becomes harder to tell what is actually happening beneath the appearance of agreement.

Softness, as I understand it through practice, is not weakness or passivity. It is staying available to the contact instead of preparing a defence against it.

I do not think a meeting is the same thing as push hands. But the practice has made me more careful about confusing control with clarity. Some of the best decisions I have seen began when somebody stopped hardening long enough for the real signal to arrive.
